I wanted to know if a small team of specialized AI agents — one to detect, one to plan, one to double-check — could handle a security incident on its own, as long as a human still had final say on anything risky. This note is my attempt to put that idea into a shape precise enough to actually check for holes, instead of just describing it.

Abstract

This is my attempt to formalize NexusGuard, a research idea I've been working on around multi-agent LLM systems for autonomous threat detection and mitigation in Kubernetes. I model it as a small set of state spaces plus three cooperating agents — a Blue Team for detection, an Orchestrator for planning the response, and a Red Team that independently verifies the outcome — all sitting behind a human approval gate. Below I define the threat-scoring model, the risk-tiered approval rule, and a bounded-retry rule for verification, and I work through an example by hand to show how the scoring actually behaves. I'm not claiming this is a production system — the point was to force the reasoning behind the design into something I could actually check for mistakes.

02

Detection & threat scoring

The Blue Team agent's whole job is turning raw telemetry into a bounded threat score plus a verdict, using a fixed detection threshold.

EQ. 2 $$\mathcal{B}(\tau_t) = (v_t,\ q_t,\ \iota_t)$$
where $q_t \in [0,100]$ is a heuristic threat score and $\iota_t$ is the set of extracted indicators of interest.
EQ. 3 $$ v_t = \begin{cases} \text{THREAT DETECTED}, & q_t \geq \theta \\ \text{SUSPICIOUS / SAFE}, & q_t < \theta \end{cases} \qquad \theta = 45 $$
The verdict is tiered in practice (e.g. CRITICAL / HIGH / MEDIUM / LOW / NONE) rather than strictly binary. The threshold $\theta=45$ is a current policy parameter: it defines the present decision boundary but is not claimed to be a universal constant.

Threat-score composition

$q_t$ is basically a sum over evidence categories — repeated auth failures, injection patterns, recon signatures, exfiltration attempts, privilege escalation, that kind of thing. Each category's contribution grows with how many times it shows up, but with diminishing returns, and it caps out at a fixed multiple of its base weight. That way no single category can run away with the whole score.

EQ. 4 $$ q_t = \min\!\left(100,\ \sum_{c\,\in\,C} \min\!\Big(w_c + (h_c-1)\cdot\max(2,\lfloor w_c/4\rfloor),\ 2w_c\Big)\right) $$
$C$ is the set of evidence categories; $w_c$ is a category's base weight; $h_c$ is how many times that category's evidence was observed.
POLICY PARAMETERS CONFIGURABLE · CALIBRATABLE

The numerical values used by the scoring rule are policy parameters, not universal mathematical constants. The equation defines how the parameters affect the score; the security policy defines their current values. They can therefore be changed and empirically calibrated without changing the mathematical structure of EQ. 4.

ParameterCurrent valueMeaningWhy it is configurable
$w_c$ category-dependent base weight assigned to evidence category $c$ different evidence categories can carry different evidentiary strength
$\theta$ 45 detection threshold in EQ. 3 can be calibrated against false-positive and false-negative behavior
$K$ fixed retry budget maximum number of verification retries in EQ. 9 can be tuned to balance recovery attempts against bounded execution
Interpretation

A value such as $w_c=12$ does not claim that 12 is an objectively correct or universal security value. It means that, under the current policy, this category starts with a base contribution of 12. What matters first is the relative ordering and rationale of category weights (for example, stronger evidence may receive a larger $w_c$). The exact numerical values are hypotheses to be validated and calibrated experimentally.

Parameter interpretation

A value such as $w_c=12$ represents the current policy value assigned to that category. The scoring structure remains unchanged if this value is modified. Numerical policy values are therefore configurable and can be calibrated against representative benign and attack scenarios.

Design principle — no double counting

One failed login, on its own, isn't an attack. A volumetric category like "repeated auth failure from one source" should only fire once an actual volumetric rule identifies the pattern, and that evidence should feed exactly one category — never get an extra bonus term stacked on top somewhere else. I learned this the hard way while testing the scoring model: it's surprisingly easy to count the same signal twice under two different names without noticing.

Illustrative worked example

Say you get a burst of repeated auth-failure log lines from one source, well past the volumetric threshold. Two categories light up here: the general "authentication failure" category, which saturates fast regardless of volume, and "repeated failure from one source," whose hit count scales with how big the burst actually is.

CategoryHit count $h_c$Contribution
Authentication failure17+24 (saturated)
Repeated-failure pattern15+36 (saturated)
Total $q_t$60 / 100 → HIGH, THREAT DETECTED
03

Mitigation selection

The Orchestrator picks a response by reasoning over the current state and the fixed risk-tier policy — there's no closed-form objective it's optimizing, it just reasons its way there.

EQ. 5
φᵢ = (toolᵢ, argsᵢ, obsᵢ)
φᵢ ∼ 𝒪LLM(st, ιt, Π, φ<i)
at = (tooln, argsn)
The Orchestrator builds its reasoning trace step by step: each step $\varphi_i$ is a (tool, arguments, observation) triple, sampled conditioned on the current state, the extracted indicators, the risk policy, and everything observed so far. The final step $a_t=(\text{tool}_n,\text{args}_n)$ is the selected mitigation. Its risk tier $\Pi(a_t)$ is looked up before anything executes.
04

Human-gated transition

This part I consider non-negotiable. Every proposed action gets a risk tier before it ever touches the system, and for the riskiest tiers a human has to decide — there's no override that lets the agent just go ahead anyway.

EQ. 6 $$\mathcal{H}(a_t,\Pi) \in \{\ \text{approved},\ \text{rejected},\ \text{pending}\ \}$$
A 3-state outcome: the action either proceeds, is blocked permanently, or waits — it never silently defaults to proceeding.
EQ. 6a $$ \Pi : \mathcal{A} \rightarrow \{\text{LOW}, \text{MEDIUM}, \text{HIGH}, \text{CRITICAL}\} $$ $$ \text{needs\_human}(a) = \begin{cases} \lnot\ \text{auto\_approve\_low}, & \Pi(a) = \text{LOW} \\ \lnot\ \text{auto\_approve\_medium}, & \Pi(a) = \text{MEDIUM} \\ \text{true}, & \Pi(a) \in \{\text{HIGH}, \text{CRITICAL}\} \end{cases} $$
$\Pi$ is a total function: every possible action has a defined risk tier, and an unrecognized action is mapped fail-safe to HIGH rather than assumed safe.
EQ. 7 $$ s_{t+1} = \begin{cases} M(s_t,\ a_t), & \mathcal{H}(a_t,\Pi) = \text{approved} \\[4pt] s_t, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} $$
The state only changes on explicit approval. Rejection or a pending decision leaves the system exactly as it was.

05

Verification & bounded retry

The Red Team agent doesn't just trust the Orchestrator's word that something worked — it independently re-derives what actually happened. And since retries are capped, a real failure escalates to a human instead of the system quietly looping forever.

EQ. 8 $$r_t = \mathcal{R}(s_{t+1},\ a_t,\ \iota_t) \in \{\ \text{SUCCESS},\ \text{FAILED}\ \}$$
EQ. 9 $$ s_t^{(k+1)} = \begin{cases} \text{END}(\text{FAILED\_ESCALATED}), & r_t^{(k)} = \text{FAILED} \ \wedge\ k = K \\[6pt] M\big(s_t^{(k)},\ \mathcal{O}(s_t^{(k)})\big), & r_t^{(k)} = \text{FAILED} \ \wedge\ k < K \\[6pt] \text{END}(\text{SUCCESS}), & r_t^{(k)} = \text{SUCCESS} \end{cases} $$
$K$ is a small, fixed retry budget. A retry re-enters the Orchestrator with the same incident context; success ends the cycle normally; an exhausted budget ends at an explicit failure state rather than retrying silently forever.

Implementation & deployment

The loop above isn't just theory — it's running as code, on a real cluster. This section says what it's actually built on and what "running" means here.

Worth being honest about here: this is still evolving, and the deterministic paths exist precisely because the LLM-driven paths aren't yet reliable enough to trust unsupervised — which is itself consistent with the whole point of the human gate in EQ. 6–7.

08

Reflection

A few honest notes on what I actually got out of formalizing this, instead of just describing it.

The numerical values in the scoring policy should be treated as configurable hypotheses rather than immutable truths. The current weights and threshold define one policy instance; a stronger empirical study would sweep or calibrate these parameters on representative benign and attack scenarios and report the resulting false-positive / false-negative trade-off.

Writing the scoring rule out as an actual formula (EQ. 4), instead of hand-waving with "the agent decides," was probably the single most useful thing I did on this project. It's the only reason I caught the double-counting mistake I mention above, where the same piece of evidence could quietly influence the score through two different paths at once. A formula makes that kind of bug impossible to miss; a paragraph of prose hides it pretty easily.

Same story with the human-gate rule. Writing it as a total function (EQ. 6a) forces me to give an explicit answer for every possible action, including ones the system has never seen before. That's actually what pushed the design toward "unknown action → treat it as HIGH risk, ask a human" instead of quietly assuming anything not on a blocklist must be fine.

The bounded-retry rule (EQ. 9) is the least exciting part of this whole thing but honestly the one that matters most in practice. Without a hard cap $K$, a workflow that keeps retrying a mitigation that isn't working can just sit there looking "in progress" forever instead of ever admitting it failed — and a clear failure that reaches a human is a lot safer than a silent retry loop that never does.

To be clear, this is still a research idea and a proof of concept, not a hardened system. What I got out of it was forcing the reasoning behind "AI agents plus human oversight" into a form precise enough that I could actually go back and check it for mistakes — and I did find mistakes, which is kind of the whole point.
